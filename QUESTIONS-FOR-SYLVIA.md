## Part 2 — bigger opportunities, no rush

A design review raised four questions I think are worth sitting with. None of them
block launch.

**1. The page proves your results, but never lets anyone watch you think.**

Your buyer's real fear isn't "will she get results." It's "will she respect what I
built." Sixteen numbers can't answer that.

The strongest thing you do in a sales call is tell someone what's wrong with their
show. Right now a stranger can't experience that anywhere on the page. One worked
example would fix it: a real finding from a real show, what you recommended, what
happened — and, the part that would actually land, **what you deliberately left alone.**
Anonymised is fine. It would do more than another statistic.

**2. There isn't one second of audio on the website of an audio producer.**

You script for the ear, you mix, you do sound design — and a visitor can't hear any
of it. Even thirty seconds of something you're proud of, with a line about why that
moment works, would say more about your judgement than a paragraph could.

**3. Two of your three objections defend you against competitors. Only one answers
the fear that actually blocks the sale.**

I've reordered them so the "what if you change everything" answer comes first. Worth
reading the three out loud and telling me if that order feels right to you.

**4. There's no price, no range, no order of magnitude.**

That was your call and I've kept it. But your buyer's stated alternative is "a cheaper
editor" — so worth asking whether staying silent builds trust here, or just gives them
an easy exit. Even "engagements typically start around X" would let someone
self-qualify before the call.

**5. The miniseries.**

You mentioned developing miniseries for both REI and Thrifty Traveler and called them
major successes, but I have no titles and no numbers. That's potentially the best proof
of your creative range on the whole page and I can't write a word of it. Which ones,
and how did they do?

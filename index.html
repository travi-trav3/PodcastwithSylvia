The problem

You're making the show. You just can't tell if it's landing.

You publish every week. The show is good. You know it's good, because you're there when it gets made.

Then the numbers come in and they don't really tell you anything.

One episode does twice the traffic of the one before it and nobody on the team can say why. You're not sure how many people actually finish. You're not sure whether YouTube is the thing now or a distraction. There's a dashboard somewhere with forty numbers on it and no one has ever explained which two matter.

So you keep making the show the way you've always made it, and you hope.

Here's the quieter part. Podcasting gets lonely. You're in a studio, or a room, or a closet, talking into a microphone, and there's almost no face to face contact with the people on the other end of it. You can go a very long time without knowing whether any of it is reaching anyone.

And in the back of your mind there's a version where the show just flattens out. It doesn't fail, exactly. It plateaus. And eventually somebody asks what it's doing for the business, and you don't have a good answer ready.

Before you ask

Fair questions.

?

"We've spent years building this. What if you come in and change everything?"

I'm not going to. I start by listening to what you've already made, and I find what's working before I say a word about what isn't. Most of what I find first is small, quick, and doesn't touch the soul of the show at all. The bigger swings come later, and you're in every one of those conversations. I'm not interested in handing you somebody else's playbook. I've seen what happens when a show gets optimized into something its listeners don't recognize, and I'd rather grow slower than do that to you.

?

"You're freelance. What if I need you and you're deep in someone else's show?"

I'm not full time and I won't pretend otherwise. I'm not the person sitting on Slack all day waiting for a ping. What I am is the person who's thought about your show before your meeting starts, who knows what your numbers did last month, and who's been doing this long enough to know which problems need a fast answer and which ones need a good one. If you need somebody at a desk from nine to five, hire that person. That's a real job and it isn't this one.

?

"An editor costs less. What am I actually getting for the difference?"

An editor makes the episode you asked for. That's a genuine skill and you should have one. But an editor isn't going to tell you your third act is where everyone's leaving, or that the format you've used for two years is why your best guest underperformed, or that there's an award category you'd win if somebody filled out the form. I'm in the tape and in the strategy at the same time, and the reason that matters is that the two things are the same problem. You can't grow a show that isn't good, and you can't tell if it's good without listening properly.
